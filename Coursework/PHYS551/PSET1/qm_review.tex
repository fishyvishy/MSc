\begin{exc}{1}
A given Hilbert space is spanned by $n$-kets $\ket{\alpha_i}$ with $i = 1,\ldots, n$. In this space I define operators $S_i$ that act on the basis vectors in the following way:
\begin{equation}
    S_i\ket{\alpha_j} = [c_j\ket{\alpha_i} + c_i\ket{\alpha_j}]|\e_{ij}| + \delta_{ij}\ket{\alpha_j}
\end{equation}
where $\e_{ij}$ is an antisymmetric tensor satisfying $\e_{ij} = -\e_{ji} - 1$, $\e_{ij}$ denote the absolute value, $\delta_{ij}$ is the Kronecker delta function and $c_i$ are some non-zero
real constants that satisfy the constraint:
\begin{equation}
    \sum_{i=1}^n c_i = -(n-1). \quad (0.2)
\end{equation}
Determine the matrix representation of an operator $S_k$ where $1 \leq k \leq n$.
\end{exc}
\begin{ans}
The matrix element $(S_i)_{mj}$ is given by:
\begin{align*}
    \bra{\alpha_m }S_i \ket{\alpha_j}
    &= (c_j \delta_{mi} + c_i \delta_{mj})|\e_{ij}| + \delta_{ij}\delta_{mj} 
\end{align*}
We consider the case $i =j$. Then, the antisymmetric metric tensor $\e_{ii} = -e_{ii} = 0$ and $\delta_{ii = 1}$ so we get 
\[ \bra{\alpha_m }S_i\ket{\alpha_j} = \delta_{ij}\delta_{mj}\]
If $i \neq j$, then $\delta_{ij} = 0$ and $|\e_{ij}| = 1$, so 
\[\bra{\alpha_m }S_i\ket{\alpha_j} = (c_j \delta_{mi} + c_i \delta_{mj})\]

Let's consider the diagonal elements $(S_i)_{mm}$. If $i = m$, then we look at the first case and see that $(S_i)_{ii} = 1$. Else, we see that $(S_i)_{mm} = (c_m\delta_{mi} + c_i \delta_{mm})|\e_{im}| = c_i$. Any off diagonal terms occur at $m \neq j$ so that $\delta_{ij}\delta_{mj} = 0$. Hence, we look at the second case and see that $(S_i)_{mj} = c_j \delta_{mi}$. So off diagonal terms occur along row $m = i$ with value $c_j$ as indexed by the column. Hence, 
\begin{align*}
    S_1 = \begin{pmatrix}
        1 & c_2 & c_3 & \ldots & c_n \\
        0 & c_1 & 0 & \ldots & 0 \\ 
        0 & 0 & c_1 & \ldots & 0\\
        \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
        0 & 0 & 0 & \ldots & c_1  
    \end{pmatrix}
    S_i = \begin{pmatrix}
        0 & 0 & 0 & \ldots & 0 \\
        0 & \ddots & 0 & \ldots & 0 \\ 
        c_1 & c_2\ldots c_{i - 1} & 1 & c_{i + 1}\ldots & c_n\\
        \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
        0 & 0 & 0 & \ldots & c_i  
    \end{pmatrix}
\end{align*}

Some $c_j$ has to be non-zero by the constraint condition. We then see that $(S_i)_{ij} = c_j$ but $(S_i)_{ji} = 0 \neq c_j^\dagger$ for $i \neq j$. Thus, $S_i$ is \textbf{not Hermitian} for any $i$. The trace of $S_i$ is the sum of the diagonal entries, which gives $(n - 1)c_i + 1$. Hence, $\tr(S_i) = (n - 1)c_i + 1$. Then, 
\[\tr(S) = \sum_{i = 1}^n\tr(S_i) = (n - 1)\sum_{i = 1}^nc_i + n = -n + n = 0\]
We also note that $S$ is \textbf{not Hermitian} since $S_{k1} = c_1$ but $S_{1k} = c_k$.
\end{ans}

\begin{exc}{2b}
Imagine due to some reason the Planck's constant $\hbar$ doubles it value i.e
$\hbar_{new} = 2\hbar$ This would mean, in terms of the original $\hbar$, all particles
would effectively behave as integer spin particles. What would you
expect to see in a Stern-Gerlach experiment if I now send silver atoms
through magnetic field oriented along $z$ direction? Give reason for your
answer.
\end{exc}
\begin{ans}
    The electron now behaves as a spin-1 particle so that eigenstates of $S_z$ are available. These will quantize the magnetic dipole moment $\mu \in [-1, 0, 1]\hbar$ so that 3 peaks will be seen on the screen. One corresponding to 0 deflection due to the $m_s = 0$ eigenstate and two peaks symmetric about the 0 deflection line corresponding to $m_s = \pm \hbar$.
\end{ans}

\begin{exc}{2c}
    I construct a Stern-Gerlach type experiment but with the following
    changes: (i) I replace the silver atoms by electrons, and I replace the
    magnetic field by a strong electric field, (ii) I replace the silver atoms
    by electrons, but I keep the magnetic field the same, (iii) I keep the
    silver atoms, but I replace the magnetic field by a strong electric field,
    and (iv) I keep the silver atoms, but I make the magnetic field constant
    along $z$-direction. Do I expect to see any effects on the screen? Give
    only qualitative answers to the questions
\end{exc}
\begin{ans}
\begin{enumerate}[(i)]
    \item The magnetic dipole moment does not interact with the electric field so the spin does not contribute. However, the electrons experience a force in the opposite direction to the electric field due to the coulomb force. So one peak emerges on the screen due to this force, which is the same for each electron.
    \item The same deflection pattern is observed as in the original experiment as the electrons have the same magnetic moment due to intrinsic spin.
    \item The magnetic moment does not interact with the electric field and since the silver atoms are electrically neutral, no deflection is seen at all.
    \item No deflection is seen as the force on the atom is given by $F_z = -\partial_z(\vec \mu \cdot \vec B) = \mu \pdif{B_z}{z}$, so if $B$ is constant in the $z$ direction, the force goes to 0.
\end{enumerate}
\end{ans}

\begin{exc}{3a}
If an operator $A$ commutes with both $J_+$ and $J_-$, show that it also
commutes with $J_z$, i.e $[A, J_z] = 0$. Here $J_{\pm}$ are the raising-lowering
operators. Does $A$ commutes with $J^2$, the total angular momentum?
\end{exc}
\begin{ans}
    Note that 
    \[J_+J_- = (J_x + iJ_y)(J_x - iJ_y) = J_x^2 -i[J_x, J_y] + J_y^2 = J_x^2 + J_y^2 + \hbar J_z\]
    Hence, 
    \[J_z = \frac1\hbar(J_+J_- - J_x^2 - J_y^2)\]
    $A$ necessarily commutes with $J_x$ and $J_y$ since these operators are formed from linear combinations of $J_\pm$. Thus, $A$ commutes with $J_z$ since it commutes with $J_\pm$ and $J_x, J_y$. 

    
    Since $A$ commutes with each of $J_x, J_y, J_z$, it commutes with $J^2$.
\end{ans}

\begin{exc}{3c}
In a certain $n$-dimensional Hilbert space I have a traceless operator
$A$ that has only two distinct eigenvalues each with certain number of
degeneracies. Show that this information can at least specify one of the
eigenvalue, say $\lambda$, in terms of $\det A$ as $\lambda \propto (\det A)^{1/n}$
\end{exc}
\begin{proof}
    Let $A$ have eigenvalues $\lambda$ and $\kappa$. If $\lambda$ has degeneracy $p$, then $\kappa$ necessarily has degeneracy $n - p$. The traceless condition gives us that 
    \[p\lambda + (n - p)\kappa = 0 \implies \kappa = \frac{p}{p - n}\lambda\]
    The determinant of $A$ is given by the product of eigenvalues (with multiplicity) so that 
    \[\det A = \lambda^p \kappa^{n - p} = \lambda^p \ab(\frac{p}{p - n}\lambda)^{n - p} = \lambda^n \ab(\frac{p}{p - n})^{n - p}\]
    Thus, 
    \[\lambda = \ab(\frac{p}{p - n})^{(n - p)/n}(\det A)^{1/n} \propto (\det A)^{1/n}\]
    as required.
\end{proof}

\begin{exc}{3d}
In a certain $n$-dimensional Hilbert space, an operator $A$ has $n $eigenvalues
$\lambda_i$ with $i = 1,\ldots, n$ such that $\lambda_i \leq \lambda_{i + 1}$. Argue that in this space expectation value of $A$ in any generic state $\ket \psi$ satisfy $\lambda_1 \leq \braket[3]{\psi}{A}{\psi}$ assuming the normalisability condition $\braket{\psi}{\psi} = 1$.
\end{exc}
\begin{ans}
Let $\ket i$ be the normalized eigenstate corresponding to eigenvalue $\lambda_i$ so that $\{\ket i\}_{i = 1, \ldots, n}$ is an orthonormal basis. Expand $\ket \psi = \sum_{m} c_n \ket{m}$. Then, 
\begin{align*}
    \braket[3]{\psi}{A}{\psi} 
    = \sum_{m,k}\bra m c_m^* A c_k \ket k
    = \sum_{m,k} c_m^* c_k\lambda_k \braket m k
    = \sum_{m} \lambda_{m} |c_m|^2 
\end{align*}
Normalization of $\psi$ dictates that $\sum_m|c_m|^2 = 1$. We then see that 
\begin{align*}
    \braket[3]{\psi}{A}{\psi} &\geq \sum_{m} \lambda_1 |c_m|^2 = \lambda_1 \sum_m |c_m|^2 = \lambda_1\\
     \braket[3]{\psi}{A}{\psi} &\leq \sum_{m} \lambda_n |c_m|^2 = \lambda_n \sum_m |c_m|^2 = \lambda_n
\end{align*}
where the inequalities hold because $\lambda_1 \leq \lambda_i \leq \lambda_n$ by assumption.
\end{ans}

\begin{exc}{4}
    Using the commutator relation $[J_x, J_y] = i\hbar J_z$ and cyclic permutations between them, show that:
    \[J_-\ket{j,m} = \hbar \sqrt{j(j + 1) - m(m - 1)}\ket{j, m -1}\]
    where $j$ is the highest eigenvalue of $J^2$ in a given Hilbert space and $J_-$ is the lowering operator. For a spin $1/2$ particle with spin pointing along $z$ direction, why isn't the total angular momentum $J^2$ simply given by $\hbar^2/4$?
\end{exc}
\begin{proof}
    First we show that $J_-\ket{j, m}$ is an eigenstate of $J_z$. Observe that 
    \begin{align*}
    [J_z, J_-] &= J_z(J_x-iJ_y) - (J_x - iJ_y)J_z \\
    &= [J_z, J_x] + i[J_y, J_z] \\
    &= i\hbar J_y -\hbar J_x = -\hbar J_-
    \end{align*}
    Now we see that 
    \begin{align*}
        J_z(J_-\ket{j, m}) &= ([J_z, J_-] + J_- J_z)\ket{j, m}\\
        &= -\hbar J_- \ket{j, m} + m\hbar J_-\ket{j, m}\\
        &= (m - 1)\hbar J_-\ket{j, m}
    \end{align*}
    so that $J_-\ket{j, m}$ is an eigenstate of $J_z$ with eigenvalue $(m - 1)\hbar$. We claim that $J_-\ket{j, m} = \alpha \ket{j, m - 1}$. We know that there can be no contributions from eigenkets with other $m$ values as they produce orthogonal eigenstates. Further, $[J_-, J^2] = [J_x - iJ_y, J_z] = 0$ as each $J_i$ commmutes with $J^2$. Thus, $J_-\ket{j, m} \neq \alpha\ket{j', m - 1}$ as this does not commute with $J^2$. Now we see that 
    \begin{align*}
        \alpha^2 &= \bra{j, m}J_-^\dagger J_-\ket{j,m}\\
        &= \bra{j, m}((J_x + iJ_y)(J_x - iJ_y))\ket{j,m}\\
        &= \braket[3]{j, m}{J_x^2 + J_y^2 - i[J_x, J_y]}{j, m}\\ 
        &= \braket[3]{j, m}{J^2 - J_z^2 + \hbar J_z}{j, m}\\
        &= j(j + 1)\hbar^2 - m^2\hbar^2 + m\hbar^2\\
        &= \hbar^2 [j(j + 1)-m(m - 1)]
    \end{align*}
    Thus, 
    \[\alpha = \hbar \sqrt{j(j + 1) - m(m - 1)}\]
    as required.


    The total angular momentum of a spin 1/2 system $(j = 1/2)$ prepared in the $m = 1/2$ state is $J^2\ket{1/2, 1/2} = 1/2(1/2 + 1)\hbar^2 = 3\hbar^2/4$. This is because the state can still have $S_x$ and $S_y$ components as $S_{x/y} {j, m} \neq 0$. 
\end{proof}

\begin{exc}{5a}
In a two-dimensional Hilbert space spanned by eigenstates of $J_z$ an
electron is in $\ev{+z}$ state. With what uncertainities can I measure $J_x$
and $J_y$ angular momenta? Determine the lower bound satisfied by the
product of these uncertainities. Do not derive the relation.
\end{exc}
\begin{ans}
    We have that 
    \begin{align*}
        \ev{J_x} &= \braket[3]{+z}{J_x}{+z} = \frac{\hbar}2\bra{+z} \ket{-z} = 0\\ 
        \ev{J_x^2} &= \braket[3]{+z}{J_x}{+z} =  \frac{\hbar}2\braket[3]{+z}{J_x}{-z} = \frac{\hbar^2}{4} 
    \end{align*}
    Thus, the uncertainty with which you can measure $J_x$ is 
    \[\Delta J_x = \sqrt{\ev{J_x^2} -\ev{J_x}^2} = \frac \hbar 2\]
    Similarly, 
        \begin{align*}
        \ev{J_y} &= \braket[3]{+z}{J_y}{+z} = \frac{i\hbar}2\bra{+z} \ket{-z} = 0\\ 
        \ev{J_y^2} &= \braket[3]{+z}{J_y}{+z} =  i\frac{\hbar}2\braket[3]{+z}{J_y}{-z} = -i^2\frac{\hbar^2}{4} = \frac{\hbar^2}{4} 
    \end{align*}
    So 
    \[\Delta J_y = \sqrt{\ev{J_y^2} -\ev{J_y}^2} = \frac \hbar 2\]

    The uncertainty relation gives us a lower bound on the product of these uncertainties: 
    \begin{align*}
        \Delta J_x \Delta J_y &\geq \frac 12 |\ev{J_x, J_y}| = \frac12 |i\hbar \ev{J_z}| = \frac 12 |i \hbar \frac\hbar 2| = \frac{\hbar^2}{4}
    \end{align*}
\end{ans}

\begin{exc}{6a}
In a given $n$-dimensional Hilbert space a Hermitian operator $A$ has $n$
non-degenerate eigenvalues $\lambda_k$ where $k = 1,\ldots n$. Show that a generic
operator like $A$ can alternatively be written as:
\[A = \sum_{k = 1}^n \lambda_k \ketbra{\psi_k}{\psi_k}\]
where $\ket{\psi_k}$ form the basis vectors of the Hilbert space with $k = 1,\ldots, n$ as before.
\end{exc}
\begin{ans}
    The orthonormality of the basis gives 
    \[\sum_{k = 1}^n \ketbra{\psi_k}{\psi_k} = 1\]
    Thus, 
    \[A = A\sum_{k = 1}^n \ketbra{\psi_k}{\psi_k} = \sum_{k = 1}^n A\ketbra{\psi_k}{\psi_k} = \sum_{k = 1}^n \lambda_k\ketbra{\psi_k}{\psi_k}\]
    as desired.
\end{ans}

    \end{align*}
\end{ans}




\begin{exc}{6c}
    Show that if two operators $A$ and $B$ commute and $\ket{\psi_1}$ is an eigenstate
of $A$ with an eigenvalue $\lambda$, then the state $\ket{\psi_2} = B\ket{\psi_1}$ is also an eigenstate of $A$ with the same eigenvalue. On the other hand show that
if there exists a basis of eigenvectors common to both the operators $A$
and $B$ then these two operators must commute.
\end{exc}
\begin{proof}
    If $A$ and $B$ commute, then $AB = BA$ and we see that 
    \[A\ket{\psi_2} = AB \ket{\psi_1} = BA \ket{\psi_1} = \lambda B \ket{\psi_1} = \lambda \ket{\psi_2}\]
    So $\ket{\psi_2}$ is eigenstate of $A$ with same eigenvalue.

    Let $\{\ket{m_i}\}$ be a basis of eigenstates with eigenvalues $a_i$ and $b_i$ for $A$ and $B$ respectively. First note that for an arbitrary basis vector $\ket{m_i}$, we have 
    \[AB \ket{m_i} = b_i A\ket{m_i} = a_i b_i \ket{m_i} = BA\ket{m_i}\]
    Then, for an arbitrary ket $\ket \psi = \sum_i c_i \ket{m_i}$, we have 
    \[AB \ket\psi = \sum_i c_i AB \ket{m_i} = \sum_i c_i BA \ket{m_i} = BA \ket{\psi}\]
    So $A$ and $B$ commute.
\end{proof}

    \end{equation}
\end{exc}
\begin{ans}
    We are looking at the matrix 
    \[H = \begin{pmatrix}
        E_1 + W_{11} & W_{12} \\ 
        W_{12}^* & E_2 + W_{22}
    \end{pmatrix}\]
    Let $\alpha = E_1 + W_{11} - E_2 - W_{22}$. Mathematica yields eigenvalues 
    \begin{align*}
        E_{\pm} = \frac12(E_1 + W_{11} + E_2 + W_{22} \pm \sqrt{\alpha^2 + 4|W_{21}|^2})
    \end{align*}
    and eigenvectors
    \begin{align*}
        \ket{\psi_\pm} &= (\alpha \mp \sqrt{\alpha^2 + 4|W_{21}|^2}, 2W_{21})\\ 
    \end{align*}
    Letting $\sim$ denote equal up to a multiplicative factor, we find 
    \begin{align*}
        \ket{\psi_-}&\sim \ab(1 - \sqrt{1 + \frac{4|W_{21}|^2}{\alpha^2}}, \frac{2W_{21}}{\alpha})\\
        & \sim (1 - \sqrt{1 + \tan^2\theta}, \tan \theta e^{i\varphi})\\ 
        & \sim (\cos \theta - 1, \sin \theta e^{i\varphi})
    \end{align*}
    Now we normalize by dividing by $|(\cos \theta - 1, \sin \theta e^{i\varphi})|^2 = \sqrt{2 - 2\cos\theta} = \sqrt{2(1 - (\cos^2\frac\theta 2 - \sin^2\frac \theta 2))} = \sqrt{4\sin^2\frac\theta2} = 2\sin \frac \theta 2$. Then, we get 
    \begin{align*}
        \ket{\psi_-} &\sim (\frac{\cos\theta - 1}{2\sin \frac \theta 2}, \frac{2\sin \frac\theta 2 \cos \frac \theta 2}{2\sin \frac \theta 2} e^{i \varphi})\\
        &\sim (-\sin\frac \theta 2, \cos \frac\theta 2 e^{i \varphi})
    \end{align*}
    splitting the $e^{i\varphi}$ between the two components gives the desired result. The same can be done for $\ket{\psi_+}$.
\end{ans}

\begin{exc}{9c}
In a two dimensional Hilbert space I construct an operator that acting
twice on the basis vectors annihilate them. On the other hand the anti-
commutator of this operator with its adjoint behaves     as an identity
operator in this space. Can you determine the operator uniquely?
\end{exc}
\begin{ans}
No you cannot. Let $T$ be an operator with $T^2 = 0$ and $TT^\dagger + T^\dagger T = 1$. Consider the operator $\overline T = e^{i\theta}T$. Then, we see that $\overline T^2 = e^{i2\theta}T^2 = 0$ and $\{\overline T, \overline{T}^\dagger\} = e^{i\theta}T e^{-i\theta}T^\dagger + e^{-i\theta}T^\dagger e^{i\theta}T = TT^\dagger + T^\dagger T = 1$. So $T$ is uniquely determined. Even if the Hilbert Space was over a real field, $T$ is not uniquely determined. As $\frac12\begin{pmatrix}
    1 & 1 \\ 
    -1 & -1
\end{pmatrix}$ and $\frac12\begin{pmatrix}
    1 & -1 \\ 
    1 & -1
\end{pmatrix}$ both satisfy the desired properties.
\end{ans}

\begin{exc}{9d}
    A Hermitian operator $A$ is diagonisable by a Unitary operator $U$. Show
that if another operator $B$ is diagonisable by the same operator $U$ then
the operators $A$ and $B$ must commute.
\end{exc}
\begin{proof}
    By assumption, $A = UA_DU^\dagger$ and $B = UB_D U^\dagger$ where $A_D$ and $B_D$ are diagonal matrices. Note that $A_D$ and $B_D$ commute since they are diagonal. Then, we see that 
    \[AB = (UA_DU^\dagger)(UB_D U^\dagger) = UA_DB_DU^\dagger = UB_DA_DU^\dagger = BA\]
    as desired.
\end{proof}
